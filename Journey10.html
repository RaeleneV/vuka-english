Journey 10

What's Your Next Move?

"You did it. You're registered, permitted, and trading legally. So why does it suddenly feel like there's a whole new set of rules?" Getting official was step one. Staying legal while you grow, taking on staff, packaging your product, chasing bigger buyers, going online, is step two, and it catches even experienced business owners off guard. This journey walks through what changes once you start scaling, the legal groundwork most people miss, and a few creative ways to grow that don't always involve renting a second shop.

Should You Still Be a Sole Proprietor?

"You've been trading under your CIPC-registered name for a year. Is that still the right structure for what's coming next?"

Most people start as a sole proprietor or a simple private company (Pty Ltd), and that's fine for getting going. But as you grow, take on staff, sign bigger contracts, or bring in a business partner, your structure matters more. A private company (Pty Ltd) keeps your personal assets more clearly separate from business risk than a sole proprietorship does, and it's usually what bigger clients, banks, and investors expect to see before they'll sign a serious deal.

💡 QUICK TIP: You don't have to deregister and start over. You can convert or restructure through CIPC as you grow, ask them directly what changing your structure involves.

Hiring Your First Team Member, Legally

"You just hired your first helper. Besides paying them, what does the law actually require of you now?"

The moment you pay someone to work for you, even part-time or family, you become an employer in the eyes of the law. You must register for UIF (Unemployment Insurance Fund) with the Department of Employment and Labour within a few days of hiring (Unemployment Insurance Act 63 of 2001; Unemployment Insurance Contributions Act 4 of 2002), and register for COIDA (Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases) too, which covers your employee if they're hurt on the job (COIDA, Act 130 of 1993). Both apply even if you only have one employee. The Basic Conditions of Employment Act (Act 75 of 1997) also sets minimum rules on things like working hours, leave, and pay slips.

💡 QUICK TIP: Register for UIF and COIDA together when you hire your first person, most employers only think of one and get caught out by the other.

Getting Retail-Ready: What Big Buyers Actually Check

"A retail buyer says they're interested, then asks for your food safety certification. Do you have anything beyond your CoA?"

Your municipal Certificate of Acceptability gets you trading legally, but big retailers and formal distributors often want more before they'll list you: proof of consistent food safety practice, sometimes a recognised system like HACCP, and NRCS approval if your product is canned, smoked, or vacuum-packed. Many also run their own supplier audits. None of this happens overnight, so it's worth finding out what a specific buyer needs before you approach them, rather than after.

💡 QUICK TIP: Ask a potential buyer for their supplier checklist before your first meeting. It tells you exactly what to fix first, instead of guessing.

The Packaging Rule Growing Businesses Miss

"You've started packaging your sauce in your own branded bottles. Did you know that makes you legally responsible for what happens to that bottle after it's thrown away?"

Once you're a producer or brand owner putting packaging onto the market in any real volume, South Africa's Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) regulations under the National Environmental Management: Waste Act may apply to you (Waste Act 59 of 2008; EPR Regulations, GN R.1184 of 2020). In practice, this means registering with an approved Producer Responsibility Organisation (PRO) and contributing toward the collection and recycling of your packaging. Very small operations are often exempt, but the thresholds are lower than most people expect, so it's worth checking rather than assuming you're too small.

💡 QUICK TIP: Search for a Producer Responsibility Organisation in your packaging category (plastics, paper, glass, metal) and ask them directly whether your volumes require you to register.

Protecting the Name You Built

"Someone in another town starts using a name almost identical to yours. Can you actually stop them?"

Registering your business with CIPC protects your company's legal identity (Companies Act 71 of 2008), but it doesn't automatically protect your brand name, logo, or recipe name from being copied by someone else. For that, you need a trademark, registered separately through CIPC's Trade Marks division (Companies Act 71 of 2008). If your business has a name, look, or product people already recognise, a trademark is what stops a competitor from trading on your reputation.

💡 QUICK TIP: You can search the CIPC trade marks register yourself before you apply, to check your name isn't already taken.

Doors That Open With B-BBEE and a Tax Clearance

"A corporate client wants to place a big order, but first they ask for your B-BBEE certificate and a tax clearance. Do you have either?"

Many corporates and all government departments are required to buy from businesses with good Broad-Based Black Economic Empowerment (B-BBEE) standing (B-BBEE Act 53 of 2003), and from suppliers who are tax compliant. If your annual turnover is below the exempted micro-enterprise (EME) threshold, you likely qualify for an automatic B-BBEE certificate, sometimes just a sworn affidavit, at no cost. A SARS Tax Compliance Status (what used to be called a tax clearance certificate) is free to obtain online once your tax affairs are in order (SARS), and both documents are often the first thing a bigger buyer or a government tender asks for.

💡 QUICK TIP: Get your SARS Tax Compliance Status and B-BBEE affidavit sorted before you need them, not after a big opportunity is already on the table.

Selling Online? Know Your Customer Data Duties

"You start taking orders and payments through WhatsApp and a simple website. Did you know you now have legal duties around your customers' personal information?"

The moment you collect customer details like names, phone numbers, addresses, or payment information, even informally, the Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA, Act 4 of 2013) applies to you. You don't need complicated systems, but you do need to keep that information secure, only use it for what you told the customer you'd use it for, and be able to delete it if they ask. The Consumer Protection Act (Act 68 of 2008) also gives online customers rights around refunds, delivery, and clear pricing that a cash-only stall doesn't usually have to think about.

💡 QUICK TIP: Write one short line customers see when they order, saying what you'll use their details for. That alone puts you well ahead of most small businesses.

Growth Moves Most Food Businesses Overlook

"Everyone scales by opening a second stall. What if that's not actually your best option?"

Renting a second location isn't the only way to grow, and it's often the most expensive. Co-packing lets another registered, NRCS-approved facility manufacture your product at scale under your brand, so you can supply retailers without building your own factory. A cloud kitchen, cooking only for delivery from a shared commercial kitchen, cuts your rent and staffing risk compared to a second storefront. Subscription boxes and pre-order models let you produce against confirmed demand instead of guessing. And since the launch of the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), some South African food producers are exploring exporting into other African markets, though this brings its own permit and standards requirements worth researching properly before you commit.

💡 QUICK TIP: Before spending on a second physical location, cost out co-packing or a shared cloud kitchen instead. The legal and financial commitment is usually much smaller.
